The FDA's 2019 DCM Warning Changed Everything We Thought We Knew About Grain-Free Dog Food

In 2019, the FDA's DCM-grain-free dog food alert shook the pet industry. Dr. Emily Ross breaks down what it meant then-and what it still means now.

What the FDA Actually Said in 2019 (And What It Didn't)

The FDA's June and July 2019 updates named 16 dog food brands most frequently appearing in DCM case reports. The brands weren't all tiny boutique operations either. Some were well-known names with loyal customer bases. The agency had received 560 reports of DCM in dogs between January 2014 and April 2019, which was a significant jump from baseline reporting rates. Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, and Whippets were appearing in disproportionate numbers, which caught cardiologists' attention fast.

But here's the thing... the FDA was careful. It did not say grain-free food causes DCM. It said there appeared to be a signal worth investigating, particularly around diets high in peas, lentils, legume seeds, and potatoes used as primary ingredients. The suspected mechanism involved taurine deficiency or impaired taurine synthesis, though even that hypothesis hadn't been confirmed. This nuance mattered enormously, and unfortunately it got lost in the media frenzy almost immediately.

I've seen this pattern repeat itself in pet nutrition: a legitimate scientific concern gets flattened into a headline, owners panic, and the actual complexity disappears. The 2019 DCM story was a textbook case. The FDA wasn't saying grain is good and grain-free is bad. It was saying: we see something unusual and we're looking at it. That's a meaningful distinction if you're a clinician, and a nearly impossible one to communicate on Instagram. For more on this, see The FDA's 2022 Pet Food Warning List Changed How I Talk to Clients About Dog Nutrition.

Why the Pet Food Industry Was Caught Flat-Footed

The grain-free trend had been building since roughly 2007, riding the wave of ancestral diet thinking and owner concern about corn and wheat fillers. By 2019, grain-free products represented somewhere between 40 and 45 percent of dry dog food sales in some retail channels, according to industry tracking data. That's not a niche. That's nearly half the shelf. So when the FDA named specific brands publicly, the industry response was, to put it politely, defensive.

Several of the named brands pushed back with statements emphasizing that correlation isn't causation, which is technically true and also somewhat tone-deaf given that the FDA wasn't making a causal claim. Other manufacturers quietly began reformulating. Some added taurine directly to their grain-free recipes. A few started including small amounts of grain to move out of the at-risk category without calling it a recipe change. Consumers who were paying attention noticed. Many weren't.

Okay, real talk for a second: my friend tried switching her Bernese Mountain Dog off grain-free after reading about this and the dog basically staged a hunger strike for four days. She called me slightly frantic at 9pm asking if she was somehow hurting him by switching, and I had to explain that dogs are dramatic and also that a healthy dog will not starve himself over kibble. He ate on day five. She was more relieved than he was. I'm sharing this because the transition anxiety was real for a lot of owners in 2019, and I don't want to minimize that even as I say the science was pointing somewhere important.

The Taurine Question and What the Science Actually Showed

Taurine is an amino acid that dogs can synthesize on their own, unlike cats, which is why it's always been required in cat food but not historically in dog food. The 2019 DCM cases complicated that assumption. Researchers at UC Davis School of Veterinary Medicine were among the groups actively studying whether certain high-legume diets were interfering with taurine bioavailability or synthesis in dogs, particularly in large and giant breeds. Early findings suggested that the issue might not be taurine content of the food itself but how well dogs could absorb and use it given specific ingredient combinations. For more on this, see The FDA's 2024 Pet Food Sodium Warning: What It Actually Changed for Dog Owners.

This is where it gets genuinely complicated, and I want to be honest with you: we didn't have clean answers in 2019. We still don't have perfectly clean answers now. Some dogs on grain-free diets developed DCM. Many thousands didn't. Breed predisposition almost certainly played a role. Individual metabolic variation mattered. The dose of legumes as a percentage of the diet mattered. It wasn't a simple equation, and anyone who told you otherwise in 2019 was oversimplifying.

What we did know, and what shaped my clinical conversations at the time, was that several dogs in the reported cases showed cardiac improvement after diet change and taurine supplementation. That's clinically meaningful even without a confirmed mechanism. I started recommending taurine level checks for any large-breed patient on a long-term grain-free diet, especially Goldens and Dobermans. It felt like the right precaution given what we were seeing. For more context on how the FDA has continued to wrestle with ingredient safety questions in the years since, the FDA's 2021 pet food ingredient push is worth reading alongside this story.

What This Meant for Dog Owners Making Real Decisions in 2019

If you had a dog on a grain-free diet in mid-2019, the right move was not to panic. It was to have a conversation with your vet. In my practice, I started triaging by breed and diet duration. A two-year-old Chihuahua who'd been on a grain-free food for six months was not my immediate concern. A five-year-old Golden Retriever who'd been eating a pea-heavy grain-free diet for three years was a different conversation. Context always matters in clinical nutrition.

For owners who wanted to stay grain-free for documented allergy reasons, I recommended choosing formulas where legumes appeared lower in the ingredient list, not as the second or third ingredient. I also started flagging that the term "grain-free" was being used as a proxy for "healthier" in a way that simply wasn't supported. Grains like oats, brown rice, and barley are not filler ingredients in the pejorative sense. They provide fermentable fiber, B vitamins, and for most dogs, present no digestive issue whatsoever. The vilification of grain was always more marketing than medicine. For more on this, see The FDA's 2021 Pet Food Ingredient Push: What It Actually Meant for Your Dog's Bowl.

The 2019 DCM story also planted seeds for broader industry accountability conversations that would grow over the following years. If you want to see how the regulatory pressure continued building, the FDA's 2022 pet food safety warning and later the FDA's 2024 pet food sodium warning both reflect how 2019 shifted the conversation toward longer-term ingredient scrutiny rather than one-off recalls. The industry is now under a different kind of watch, and I think that's ultimately good for dogs even when it's uncomfortable for manufacturers.

My take, and I'll own it clearly: the 2019 DCM alert was handled imperfectly by everyone involved, including the FDA, the industry, and honestly some veterinarians who swung too hard in either direction. But the underlying instinct, to pay closer attention to what's actually in these foods and how novel ingredient combinations affect cardiac health over years, was right. Your dog can't read the bag. You're the one making this call for them, and having more information, even messy information, is better than having none.
