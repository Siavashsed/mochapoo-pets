Dog Temperament: What First-Time Owners Really Need to Know

By Dr. Emily Ross

Photo by Caio / Pexels

Every few years, a mixed breed quietly wins over the dog-owning public before the wider veterinary community even catches up. The dog, a three-way cross of Maltese, Chihuahua, and Poodle, is doing exactly that right now. I've seen a noticeable uptick in dog patients at my practice over the past two years, and the questions first-time owners bring in are remarkably consistent: Why is my dog so attached to me? Why does she bark at absolutely everything? Is this normal? The short answer is yes. The longer answer is what this article is about.

My position is straightforward: dogs are genuinely wonderful dogs for the right owner, but they are not the easygoing, low-maintenance companions some breeders imply they are. Their temperament is a product of three breeds with strong personalities, and understanding that heritage isn't optional. It's the whole game. If you go in prepared, you'll have one of the most affectionate, entertaining dogs you've ever owned. If you don't, you'll spend the next twelve years wondering what went wrong.

Where That Personality Actually Comes From

To understand a dog's temperament, you have to respect what each parent breed contributes. The Maltese brings a centuries-old companion instinct, a dog literally bred to sit in laps and bond deeply with one person. The Chihuahua adds alertness, loyalty that borders on possessiveness, and a surprisingly bold attitude packed into a tiny frame. And the Poodle, even in its miniature or toy form, brings intelligence, sensitivity, and a strong need for mental engagement. Stack those three together and you get a dog that is clever, devoted, and very much aware of everything happening around it.

This isn't a random mix of traits. Research from 2019 published in PLOS Genetics found that behavioral tendencies in dogs are significantly heritable, with traits like trainability and social bonding showing heritability estimates above 50 percent. That means the dog's intensity isn't a quirk of one individual dog. It's baked in at a population level. When I explain this to new owners, something visibly shifts. They stop treating the behavior as a problem to fix and start treating it as a characteristic to work with.

What surprises most first-time dog owners is just how emotionally tuned-in these dogs are. They read your mood. They notice when you're stressed. I've had clients tell me their dog started pacing before a family argument was even fully underway. That sensitivity is a gift if you channel it into training. It becomes a liability if the dog has no structure and starts making its own decisions about what's threatening and what isn't.

The Attachment Question: Velcro Dog or Separation Anxiety Waiting to Happen

Dogs form intense bonds. That's one of their most celebrated traits, and it's also the one most likely to cause problems if left unmanaged. There's a real difference between a dog that loves being near you and a dog that cannot tolerate your absence. In my work with small companion breeds, I've seen that line crossed more often with dogs than with almost any other mix I treat regularly. The signs are subtle at first: whining when you go to another room, refusing to eat when you're not home, destructive behavior that looks like boredom but is actually distress.

The misconception I hear constantly is that crate training or teaching alone-time is somehow unkind to a breed that was designed to be a companion. It's the opposite. A dog that has never learned to self-soothe is not a happy dog. It's an anxious one. Short, positive separation exercises started early, even just stepping out of the room for two minutes and returning calmly, build the psychological resilience these dogs need. And starting early matters. The critical socialization window closes around 12 to 16 weeks, and habits formed before that point are significantly harder to reshape later.

So if you're bringing home a dog puppy and you work from home, that's both an advantage and a risk. The advantage is that early bonding happens naturally. The risk is that the puppy never learns the world doesn't end when you leave the room. Build in intentional separation from week one. Your future self will be grateful.

Energy, Intelligence, and Why Boredom Is the Real Enemy

People assume small dogs don't need much exercise. Dogs will cheerfully prove that assumption wrong. Yes, a 20-minute walk covers their physical baseline. But the Poodle influence means their brain needs just as much stimulation as their legs. A physically tired dog that hasn't had any mental engagement is still going to redecorate your couch cushions. Puzzle feeders, short training sessions, scent games, even hide-and-seek with treats around the house: these aren't luxuries. They're maintenance.

Training also doubles as enrichment for this breed. Dogs pick up commands quickly, sometimes alarmingly so. But they also get bored with repetition. When I tested a simple rotation of three new tricks per week with a client's 8-month-old dog, the behavioral complaints dropped significantly within a month. The dog wasn't calmer because it was tired. It was calmer because it had a job. Small dogs with sharp minds need to feel useful, and training gives them that sense of purpose without requiring acres of yard space.

There is genuine debate in the training community about whether dogs respond better to reward-based methods alone or whether they occasionally need firmer boundary-setting. My position is that positive reinforcement works, but it has to be consistent. Inconsistency is what creates a manipulative, demanding small dog. And no one wants that. The owners who get the best results are the ones who are clear and calm every single time, not just when they feel like it.

What First-Time Owners Get Wrong in the First Six Months

The biggest mistake I see is treating a dog like a stuffed animal instead of a dog. They're small and adorable and easy to carry everywhere, which means owners unconsciously skip the socialization work they'd never skip with a Labrador. But a dog that hasn't been exposed to strangers, children, other dogs, traffic, and the general chaos of the world by the time it's four months old will fill in those blanks with fear. And fear in a small dog usually presents as barking, snapping, or resource-guarding, behaviors that are very hard to walk back once they're established.

The second thing people underestimate is noise reactivity. All three parent breeds have watchdog tendencies. The dog inherits a very alert, vocal disposition as a result. This isn't aggression. But it can become a serious quality-of-life issue in apartment settings if it's not addressed early. Desensitization, rewarding calm behavior in the presence of triggers, and avoiding the reflexive "it's okay, it's okay" reassurance that actually reinforces the anxious response: these are the tools that work. Comfort during a bark-fest tells the dog its alarm was warranted.

But here's what I want first-time owners to hold onto: the same traits that make dogs challenging in the early months are exactly what make them extraordinary long-term companions. Their intensity of feeling, their intelligence, their loyalty. These aren't bugs. They're features. The six-month investment in proper socialization and training pays dividends for twelve or more years. I've watched clients go from overwhelmed and second-guessing themselves to absolutely devoted to a dog they can't imagine living without. That turnaround is real, and it's available to anyone willing to put in the early work.

If you take one thing from this article, make it this: spend the next seven days introducing your dog to three new experiences it hasn't encountered yet, a different walking route, a visit to a pet-friendly store, a neighbor it hasn't met. Keep the encounters short, keep your energy calm, and let the dog set the pace. That kind of deliberate, low-pressure exposure, done consistently in the first few months, shapes a temperament that will serve both of you for the life of the dog. You don't need to be a professional trainer. You just need to show up with intention.
