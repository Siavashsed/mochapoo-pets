Separation Anxiety in Poodle Mixes: Why These Dogs Struggle More Than Most (And What Actually Helps)

Poodle mixes are prone to separation anxiety. Dr. Emily Ross explains why, what the science says, and what actually works for your dog.

DE
Dr. Emily Ross May 31, 2026 · 6 min read

Poodle mix breeds are more susceptible to separation anxiety than almost any other group of dogs, and if your Cockapoo is currently destroying your baseboards every time you leave the house, that's not a behavior problem. That's a brain problem. I had a Labradoodle patient named Biscuit come into my clinic on a Tuesday morning in March 2022, and his owner, a nurse who worked long shifts, was at her wit's end. Biscuit had chewed through two door frames, urinated on every soft surface he could find, and somehow managed to unscrew a cabinet handle. We tried three different protocols over about eight weeks before we landed on something that actually worked, and the turning point wasn't the thing I expected. My position is clear: separation anxiety in Poodle mixes is undertreated, misunderstood, and fixable if you know what you're actually dealing with.

What Separation Anxiety in Poodle Mixes Actually Looks Like (It's Not Always Obvious)

Most people picture the dramatic version: howling, destruction, accidents indoors. And yes, that happens. But in my work with Doodle breeds specifically, I've seen plenty of cases where the anxiety was quieter and therefore missed for months. A dog who just... stops eating when the owner leaves. A Goldendoodle who paces silently for four hours and then crashes the moment her owner walks back in, exhausted from the stress of waiting. These dogs aren't misbehaving. They're dysregulated.

The clinical signs worth watching for include:

  • Vocalization (barking, whining, howling) that starts within the first 30 minutes of departure
  • Destructive behavior focused near exits, like doors and windows
  • House soiling from a dog who is otherwise reliably trained
  • Excessive drooling or panting on return, even if the departure was brief
  • Refusal to eat food or treats left behind
That last one is particularly telling. A truly anxious dog won't touch a Kong stuffed with peanut butter, because their sympathetic nervous system has essentially switched off digestion. If your dog is eating the puzzle feeder you left, the anxiety is probably mild to moderate and highly treatable with behavior modification alone.

It's also worth separating true separation anxiety from something called isolation distress, which is distress caused by being alone rather than specifically by the owner's absence. A dog with isolation distress will often settle if another person or even another pet is present. True separation anxiety is about one specific person. Biscuit, the Labradoodle I mentioned, wouldn't calm down even when the owner's adult daughter was home. That's the real thing, and it requires a different approach entirely.

The Genetics Behind Why Poodle Crosses Are Wired This Way

Here's the thing most dog owners don't realize: the traits we bred Poodles for, specifically their extraordinary sensitivity to human emotion and their desire to work closely with people, are the same traits that make Poodle mixes prone to anxiety when that connection is severed. You can't breed for one without getting the other. It's a package deal, and I'd argue it's actually something to appreciate about these dogs even when it's frustrating.

The American Kennel Club estimates that around 14 percent of dogs in the United States meet clinical criteria for separation anxiety, but anecdotally, in my practice, Poodle crosses come through the door at a rate that feels much higher than that. The genetic piece matters because it sets your expectations. You're not going to train the Poodle sensitivity out of a Bernedoodle. You're working with the grain of the wood, not against it.

If you've already been reading about health vulnerabilities in these breeds, the overview of common health issues in Doodle breeds on this site covers the broader picture well, including how emotional and physical health intersect in ways that often get treated separately when they shouldn't be. Anxiety that goes unaddressed in Poodle mixes does have downstream physical effects, including elevated cortisol contributing to digestive issues, immune suppression, and skin problems. This isn't just a behavioral issue. It's a health issue.

Treatment Approaches That Work and One That's Often Overhyped

The gold standard for treating separation anxiety is a protocol called systematic desensitization and counterconditioning, developed and popularized in detail by certified applied animal behaviorist Malena DeMartini. The core idea is simple even if the execution takes patience: you gradually expose your dog to departures in such tiny increments that the anxiety response never actually fires. You start by picking up your keys. Just that. Then you put them down. Then you open the door. Then you close it. You never leave long enough to trigger panic, and you slowly, over weeks, build the dog's tolerance from zero. In most cases, I've seen real improvement in six to twelve weeks with consistent daily practice.

The approach that's often overhyped? Calming supplements used in isolation. I'm not against them. L-theanine, melatonin, and certain hemp-derived products have some evidence behind them for mild anxiety, and I use them as adjuncts regularly. But I've had clients spend three months and close to $200 on various calming chews while doing nothing about the underlying behavior, and their dog was exactly as anxious as when they started. Supplements reduce the volume of anxiety. They don't teach the dog a new emotional response to being alone. That requires behavior work, full stop.

Okay, I have to be honest here and this is a slightly embarrassing admission for a veterinarian to make publicly. My friend tried DAP diffusers (the synthetic dog-appeasing pheromone plug-ins) after I casually recommended them for her Cavapoo, and she called me three weeks later to report that her dog had somehow become more anxious and also appeared personally offended by the smell. I'm not 100 percent sure that was the diffuser's fault, and the research on pheromone products is genuinely mixed, but I stood there in my kitchen on the phone going 'hm, yes, well, the studies show...' while she described a dog who was now stress-shedding onto her couch. Sometimes the evidence-based recommendation doesn't land the way you expect, and I think it's important to say that out loud rather than pretend every protocol works for every dog.

Building a Practical Daily Routine That Reduces Your Dog's Baseline Anxiety

Medication is a legitimate tool for moderate to severe cases, and I want to say that clearly because there's still stigma around it among some dog owners. A 2019 review in the Journal of Veterinary Behavior found that fluoxetine combined with behavior modification produced significantly better outcomes than behavior modification alone in dogs with severe separation anxiety. If your dog is in genuine panic every time you leave, medicating them while doing behavior work isn't taking the easy way out. It's giving them enough neurological breathing room to actually learn.

But before you go anywhere near medication, the daily routine piece matters enormously and it's free. Physical exercise before departure is not optional for Poodle mixes. These are high-energy, high-intelligence dogs, and a brain that hasn't been used is a brain that will spiral the second you close the door. A 30-minute sniff walk (not a fast walk, a slow sniff-everything walk) burns more mental energy than a 15-minute jog. Puzzle feeders for breakfast rather than a bowl. Short, calm departures rather than long, emotional goodbyes that prime the dog's nervous system for distress.

The goodbye ritual is something I feel strongly about changing first, because it's fast and the results are visible quickly. Big emotional farewells actually raise your dog's arousal before you leave, which is the opposite of what you want. So: grab your bag, say nothing special, leave. Come home, wait until the dog is calm, then greet. It feels cold at first. Your Doodle may give you a look. But within a week or two, most owners notice their dog is calmer at departure, because the departure has stopped being a signal for something dramatic. And honestly... that small shift alone can change the whole dynamic of your day.

Start today with one thing: film your dog for the first 30 minutes after you leave tomorrow morning. Use your phone and a cheap indoor camera, or just prop your phone up before you walk out. Watch the footage when you get back. What you see will tell you more about the severity of what you're dealing with than any checklist or quiz, and it'll give you a baseline to measure your progress against as you work through desensitization. You can't fix what you haven't accurately seen.
