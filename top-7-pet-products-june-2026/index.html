The Week's Top 7 Pet Products Worth Knowing About Right Now (June 2026)

Dr. Emily Ross ranks June 2026's top 7 trending pet products and innovations. Science-backed picks with honest advice on who each one is really for.

DE
Dr. Emily Ross June 07, 2026 · 6 min read

Not every trending product deserves your money, and this week's crop is a genuinely mixed bag. I spent part of last Tuesday afternoon going through the latest submissions from brands and cross-referencing them against recent clinical data, because honestly, some of what's circulating right now would make my nutrition professor weep. But a handful of these are legitimately impressive, and I think you deserve a straight answer on which is which.

If you followed last month's Top 7 roundup, you'll notice a theme continuing here: the best innovations this cycle aren't flashier toys or fancier packaging. They're products solving real, boring, unglamorous problems that vets like me have been flagging for years. Gut health. Joint inflammation in younger dogs. Ingredient transparency. These aren't headline-grabbing topics, but they matter more than any glow-in-the-dark collar ever will.

Numbers 7 Through 5: Solid Picks With One Caveat Each

#7: Zuma Raw Frozen Toppers (Single-Protein, USDA-Verified). Raw feeding is one of the most genuinely debated topics in veterinary nutrition right now, and I'm not going to pretend there's consensus. But Zuma's new single-protein frozen toppers earn their spot here because of one thing: USDA verification on every batch, which is rarer than it should be. The FDA has documented significant contamination risks in raw pet food, and most brands simply don't do the pathogen testing to back up their claims. Zuma does. Best for: owners already committed to raw feeding who want a lower-risk entry point. Not for households with immunocompromised members or young children.

#6: BarkBalance Probiotic Chews (Postbiotic Formula). The shift from probiotics to postbiotics is one I've been watching for a couple of years. Where probiotics deliver live bacteria (which have to survive the manufacturing process, packaging, and your dog's stomach acid), postbiotics deliver the beneficial byproducts those bacteria produce. A 2022 review in Nutrients found postbiotic supplementation showed measurable improvements in gut barrier function in companion animals. BarkBalance's formula includes butyrate and short-chain fatty acids at clinically meaningful concentrations. Best for: dogs with recurring loose stools or sensitive stomachs. Caveat: the salmon flavor is apparently polarizing. My own dog, a deeply opinionated beagle named Clementine, refused the first batch entirely and I had to hide them inside a spoonful of peanut butter like some kind of criminal.

#5: PetSafe Smart Feeder Pro 2.0. Automated feeders aren't new, but this update fixes the two things that made the original frustrating: the portion accuracy (now within 2 grams) and the app reliability. In my work with clients managing dogs on weight-loss protocols, inconsistent portion sizes are the single biggest reason diet plans stall. This feeder also logs feeding times and portions to a shareable health dashboard, which means you can actually show your vet real data instead of a rough estimate. Best for: multi-person households where feeding responsibility is shared and nobody's quite sure if the dog already ate. Which, let's be honest, is most households. For more on this, see The Week's Top 7 Pet Products Worth Knowing About Right Now (May 2026).

Numbers 4 and 3: Two Products I'd Actually Recommend to My Own Clients

#4: Ollie Vet-Formulated Fresh Food Subscription (Updated June 2026 Formula). Fresh food subscriptions have been around long enough that I'm no longer impressed by the concept alone. What I'm looking at now is formulation quality, and Ollie's updated June 2026 recipes are genuinely worth attention. They've brought fiber ratios in line with AAFCO's 2025 updated guidelines and reduced sodium levels in their senior formulas by around 18%, which matters a lot for older dogs with early cardiac changes. If you're thinking about grain-free options, please read about the FDA's 2019 DCM warning that changed how we think about grain-free dog food before committing to any subscription. Ollie's updated recipes are not grain-free, which is part of why I'm comfortable ranking them this high.

#3: Vetericyn Plus Antimicrobial Wound Spray (New Ophthalmology Formula). Okay, I know. An eye wash spray doesn't sound exciting. But this new ophthalmic version of Vetericyn's hypochlorous acid formula is one of those products where the science is really clean. Hypochlorous acid at the right concentration kills bacteria, fungi, and some viruses without damaging corneal tissue, and the new formula is pH-balanced specifically for canine ocular use. I've seen too many dogs come in with minor eye irritation that became serious infections because owners used the wrong product or waited too long. This one is shelf-stable, genuinely safe, and fast-acting. Best for: any dog prone to eye discharge, debris accumulation, or the kind of face-fold issues common in brachycephalic breeds. Not a replacement for a vet visit if symptoms persist beyond 48 hours.

The Top 2: My Picks for Most Significant Innovations This Week

#2: Purina Pro Plan Veterinary Diets OM Hydrolyzed Treat Line. Here's where I'll take a clear editorial position: hydrolyzed protein treats have been underserved as a category for years. Dogs with food allergies have had prescription food options, but almost nothing in the treat space that meets the same standard. Purina's new OM hydrolyzed line changes that. The proteins are broken down to a molecular weight below the threshold that typically triggers immune responses, which means dogs with diagnosed chicken or beef allergies can actually have a treat that won't undo weeks of elimination diet work. A 2020 study in the Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine confirmed that hydrolyzed protein diets significantly reduced clinical signs in dogs with cutaneous adverse food reactions. These treats extend that same principle. Best for: dogs on elimination diets or with confirmed food allergies. Your vet should still sign off before you add anything new during an active elimination trial.

And now, honestly, a small confession before we get to number one. My colleague Sarah tried the number two pick on her golden retriever, Harvey, who has a beef allergy so dramatic it's practically a personality trait. She called me at 7am on a Saturday to report that Harvey ate three treats without scratching once. I'm not saying that's a clinical trial. I'm saying it made her cry a little. I'm not 100% sure this works for every dog with food sensitivities, because allergies are genuinely individual and unpredictable, but the mechanism is sound and the early anecdotal response has been encouraging across more than just Harvey. For more on this, see The FDA's 2021 Pet Food Ingredient Push: What It Actually Meant for Your Dog's Bowl.

#1: Companion AI Health Monitor (Wearable + Subscription Platform, v3.0). Wearable health monitors for pets have existed for a while, but most of them track activity and call it health data. Companion's v3.0 does something meaningfully different: it uses a combination of heart rate variability, respiratory rate during rest, and skin surface temperature to generate what they're calling a Daily Vitals Score. The algorithm was trained on data from over 40,000 dogs across 14 breeds. Is it a diagnostic tool? No, and I want to be clear about that. But as an early-warning system that prompts owners to bring a dog in before a small problem becomes an emergency, it fills a real gap. If you've ever been on the fence about whether your dog is actually sick or just being dramatic... this gives you a third data point. Best for: senior dogs, dogs with chronic conditions, and anxious pet parents who need something to do besides worry.

One Ingredient Trend to Watch, and One to Be Skeptical Of

Several products this week are leaning hard on omega-3 phospholipid complexes as a differentiator. The science here is real: krill-sourced omega-3s in phospholipid form are absorbed more efficiently than the triglyceride form found in most fish oils. If you're currently using a standard fish oil supplement, it's worth knowing that you might get equivalent benefit from a lower dose of a phospholipid-form product. Not every brand is using meaningful concentrations, so check the EPA and DHA milligrams per serving, not just the total omega-3 figure.

On the skeptical side: "gut microbiome testing" kits for dogs are proliferating fast, and I'm genuinely uncertain the science is there yet to justify the price (most kits run between $80 and $150). The human microbiome research is still evolving, and the canine equivalent is even less settled. These tests might eventually be useful, but right now the actionable guidance they produce is often too vague to change what you'd actually feed your dog. I'd hold off. Check back in 18 months.

For anyone thinking about how ingredient quality claims get evaluated, the conversation I've had the most this year with clients still traces back to the FDA's 2022 pet food safety warning and what it actually changed about how I counsel clients. The short version: brand reputation alone isn't enough. Knowing what to ask for on a label matters more than most people realize. For more on this, see The FDA's 2022 Pet Food Warning List Changed How I Talk to Clients About Dog Nutrition.

Before you buy anything from this list, pick one product that addresses an actual problem your dog has right now, not the most interesting-sounding one. The Purina hydrolyzed treats and the BarkBalance chews both have narrow, specific ideal users. If your dog doesn't have food allergies or gut issues, skip them and look at the feeder or the wearable monitor instead. Match the product to the problem. That's the whole job.
