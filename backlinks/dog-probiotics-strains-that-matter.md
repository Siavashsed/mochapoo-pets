**Title:** PSA: the billions on a dog probiotic label matter less than whether it names the strain

I went down a rabbit hole after spending way too much on probiotic chews for my dog that did nothing. What finally clicked for me: the CFU count on the front of the bag (5 billion! 10 billion!) tells you very little. What matters is the full strain name, which has three parts: genus, species, and a strain code. "Lactobacillus acidophilus" is just a species. "Enterococcus faecium SF68" is a strain, and that code is what the published dog studies actually tested.

Two strains of the same species can behave completely differently. So a product that only lists species names is asking you to trust that whatever they put in works. A lot of shelf products also blend 6 to 10 species with no strain codes. It sounds impressive, but it gives you nothing to look up.

My quick checklist now: the label lists strain codes, there is at least one published trial in dogs for that strain, and the CFU is guaranteed through the expiry date, not just at manufacture.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I choose a probiotic for my dog that actually works?

Start with the label, not the billions. The most useful thing you can check is whether the product names the exact strain of each bacterium.

A complete strain name has three parts: genus, species, and a strain designation. Enterococcus faecium is a species. Enterococcus faecium SF68 is a strain. That matters because research is done on strains, and two strains of the same species can behave very differently in the gut. If a label only lists species, you can't connect what's in the jar to any study.

Here is the checklist I use with clients:

1. Named strains with codes. If they aren't on the label or the brand's website, move on.
2. Published research in dogs. Human data doesn't always carry over. A few strains, SF68 being the best known, have controlled trials in dogs.
3. CFU guaranteed through the expiry date, not "at time of manufacture." Probiotics die off on the shelf, especially in soft chews exposed to heat.
4. Fewer strains, done well. A blend of ten species without strain codes looks impressive but gives you nothing to verify.
5. A clear dose by body weight.

A real example: in 2021 I saw a pug who had been on three different store-shelf chews, about $140 worth, with no change in his stools. We switched him to a single-strain product with published trials, and his stools firmed up within four days. One dog proves nothing on its own, but it matches what the research suggests.

One caution: a probiotic supports the gut but doesn't treat illness. If your dog has diarrhea lasting more than two or three days, blood in the stool, vomiting or lethargy, see your vet before trying any supplement.
