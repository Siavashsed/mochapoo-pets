## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What dog products are actually worth buying, and what should I spend money on first?

As a veterinarian, my honest answer is to spend on prevention before novelty, and the clearest example is dental care.

Dental disease is among the most common conditions I see in dogs, and it's easy to miss. Owners notice bad breath and shrug it off. By the time there's visible gum recession, loose teeth, or reluctance to chew, the problem has usually been building for a long while. I learned this the uncomfortable way with my own nine-year-old beagle mix, Waffles. I'd noticed his gums receding for about a year and kept telling myself it was age. It wasn't, and it took me far too long to admit it.

So if you only have a small budget this week, I'd put roughly $25 toward quality dental chews before I'd put $150 toward any gadget. A few things to look for: chews sized appropriately for your dog, a texture that encourages real chewing rather than gulping, and ideally a product that carries recognition from a veterinary oral health council. Always supervise, and choose a size that can't be swallowed whole.

That doesn't mean gadgets are a waste. Some earn their price, but usually only for a specific need, such as a dog with a particular medical or behavioral issue, and only if you'll use them consistently. A device that lives in a drawer helps no one.

A few general rules I give clients:

1. Prevention beats treatment, both for your dog's comfort and your wallet.
2. Buy for a problem your dog actually has.
3. Consistency matters more than price.
4. Talk to your vet before changing diet or adding supplements, especially for seniors.
