I've worked with a lot of poodle mixes, and Mochapoos stand out for how fast they learn. That's the good news and the problem. A few years ago I trained a 14-week-old named Biscuit. Early on, his owner used a sharp leash pop and a loud 'no' whenever he pulled. Within a couple of weeks he stopped pulling, but he also started flinching when people reached for him and refused to take treats outdoors. He had learned that walks were stressful, which is not what anyone intended.

We switched to rewards: freeze-dried liver for checking in, a calm sit before every door, and a quick end to any session that went sideways. Within a few weeks he was walking loose-leash and actually looking up at his owner for guidance.

What I keep seeing is that these dogs are so tuned in to people that harsh corrections land harder than owners expect. Short sessions, high-value treats and consistency seem to beat everything else.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is the best way to train a Mochapoo puppy, and does punishment work?

As a veterinarian, I can tell you that reward-based training is the best approach for a Mochapoo, and punishment-based methods usually backfire within a few weeks.

Mochapoos combine poodle intelligence with cocker spaniel sensitivity. They are people-obsessed and learn very quickly, which means they absorb everything you teach, including lessons you never intended. A sharp leash pop or a harsh scolding might stop a behaviour in the moment, but the dog often links the unpleasantness to the situation, not to the specific action. I have seen puppies become wary of walks, flinch at raised hands, or refuse food outdoors after only a couple of weeks of corrections.

What works instead:

1. Keep sessions short. Five minutes, a few times a day, is better than one long session. Young puppies tire mentally fast.

2. Use high-value rewards. Small pieces of freeze-dried liver or chicken beat ordinary kibble for new skills.

3. Reward immediately. Mark the behaviour within a second or two so the dog understands exactly what earned the treat.

4. Build routines. A calm sit before meals, doors and leash-ups teaches impulse control without any conflict.

5. End on a win. If a session goes badly, ask for something easy, reward it and stop.

6. Be consistent. Everyone in the household should use the same cues and rules.

7. Manage the environment. Prevent rehearsal of unwanted behaviours with gates, leashes and supervision, rather than correcting after the fact.

Socialisation matters too. Expose your puppy to new people, surfaces and sounds gently, always letting the dog set the pace.
